My uncle said there was one fish in that pool, and that it was the same fish it had always been.

He said this the way he said everything — while doing something else, so that you could not tell whether you were being taught or merely spoken near. He was splicing a rope. He was always splicing a rope, though I never once saw him on a boat.

"The same fish," I said. "Since when?"

"Since it started."

I was eleven and had recently discovered the word *literally*, which I deployed the way a small country deploys a new weapon. I explained about lifespans. I explained about the sea, and spawning, and dying afterwards, which I had read in a book with cross-sections.

He let me get all the way to the end.

"That's a great deal of information," he said, "about a different fish."

We went down to the pool that evening because he had decided, without announcing it, that I should see. The water there goes very still under the hazels, and there is a shelf of rock where the current pauses, and the whole place has the quality of a room somebody has just left.

We didn't fish. He never fished, either.

We sat until the midges got serious, and just at the end, when the light was going amber and the surface had turned to hammered tin, something moved out in the middle. Not a jump. A shoulder of water, rolling over on itself, and then gone.

I have thought about that evening for thirty years, and I have arrived at this: he was not lying to me, and I was not wrong. There was a fish in that pool that had been there since it started. It was made of every summer anyone in my family had ever spent looking at that water, and it was fed on all the things they didn't say, and it was, in the way that matters, entirely real.

He finished the splice on the walk home. He gave me the rope. I still have it.
